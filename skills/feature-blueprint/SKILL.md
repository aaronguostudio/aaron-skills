# Shape

Help the agent turn a request into a delivery its next receiver can understand,
inspect and use. Connect what people need to the result they should experience,
then choose the expression that supports the next conversation, decision or
execution step. There are no fixed phases or required document counts. Use the
default proposal-page template below for substantial web proposals, adapting its
content to the project while preserving its section labels. Honor another
requested medium or existing format.
The core skill has no runtime or service dependency.

**Lead the receiver through the reasoning.** Establish the concrete situation,
the consequential question and the recommendation with its reason. Use a
source-grounded example to connect the need, the proposed experience and the
decision that follows. Each part should add a reason, evidence, a consequence or
a useful choice. Choose the reading order for the receiver; Why, What and How
can guide the thinking without becoming compulsory headings. For a proposal
page, keep the stable section names below; use project-specific language in the
content and screen names. For other media, use their existing conventions. Keep the language plain and specific. Replace
generic promises such as better visibility with the information, judgment or
action that would change. Do not invent an incident, result or stakeholder
answer to make a story persuasive.

Open with the requested change in one or two plain sentences; a long meeting can
be grouped into a few distinct requests. Titles should name the requirement or
content directly. Omit slogan-like wording, decorative section eyebrows,
breadcrumb-style edition labels and forced all-caps text. Keep material
uncertainty beside the affected claim rather than adding a general disclaimer.

The following are interacting habits. Move among them as the problem develops;
use what changes the understanding and leave out what does not.

**Stay close to the intent.** Understand who is affected, what is difficult,
why it matters and what improvement would look like. Treat an initial solution
as a useful starting point when its purpose is unclear. Respect decisions and
constraints already supplied; a clear request needs no artificial discovery.
When interpretation matters, briefly play back the need and its consequence in
words the user can correct. Mark an inferred motive as a hypothesis; do not
announce that you have found their "real problem" or replace their chosen goal.

When a capability changes a workflow, connect its business purpose to actual use:
who supplies and maintains the information, what prompts an update, where it is
reviewed and what someone can do differently afterward. Keep an unagreed role,
cadence or response open rather than filling the gap with a plausible routine.

**Seek context with a purpose.** Follow a question into the relevant code,
meetings, past decisions or other authorized sources. Retrieve what could change
the interpretation or next action. Resolve accessible technical facts from the
project before asking the user. Stop collecting when further material is
unlikely to change the decision; return to a source when a new gap appears.

**Simplify without erasing uncertainty.** Connect the facts into the important
relationships, constraints and tensions. Keep observations, proposals,
inferences and confirmed decisions distinct. Preserve consequential exceptions
and conflicting evidence. A tidy summary is still provisional when its central
assumptions have not been tested. Reconsider the framing when evidence does not fit.
When source wording is ambiguous, inspect the exact relevant line in each source
before claiming one resolves the other. A plausible correction is an interpretation,
not an observed fact. Keep a short quote and source pointer in local detail, state
the interpretation as proposed, and carry its unresolved status into dependent
requirements, designs and handoffs. Repeated ambiguous wording is still unresolved;
see [source wording](references/workflow.md#check-the-words-before-correcting-them).

**Choose the inquiry that changes the next action.** Investigate an accessible
fact; ask someone who owns a preference or policy; use a sketch or experiment
when feasibility or experience is uncertain. Ask for a concrete recent incident
when the need is still vague. For a genuine choice, explain the consequence and
offer a recommendation with its tradeoff. Do not turn an open exploration into
a forced menu. Make a follow-up easy to answer, concentrating on what could
change scope, priority, behavior or success. Avoid quotas and repeated questions.
Keep independent reversible work moving while a necessary answer is pending.

**Think visibly, without waiting to be asked.** Proactively create a visual that
helps the user see, compare or correct the current idea; do not stop at offering
to make one. Ground it in the actual context. Even a small interface change can
benefit from an in-context design and its resulting state. A new app may call for
connected core screens and interactions; a rule or process may become clearer
as a flow or state diagram. Choose the medium freely, including native designs,
sketches, prototypes or motion when useful. Use available native capabilities
and existing project tools, respecting the user's medium and creative direction.

Let expression participate early in discovery. Make consequential assumptions
visible, and leave uncertain details open through roughness, alternatives or a
brief annotation. Preserve room for imagination; keep explanatory text selective.
Do not force a page, dashboard, workbench or reusable layout onto every problem.
Match fidelity and scope to the next decision rather than designing every possible
screen. When a visual would only repeat a clear answer or conflict with the user's
constraints, keep the response direct. If a medium is unavailable, choose a useful
available form and state the limitation rather than claiming an integration.

Give a substantial design its own usable viewing space. Show the actual screens,
states and transitions through a coherent example; make relationships visible
through placement and connections. Select fidelity, scale and navigation so the
receiver can inspect the important result without reconstructing it from prose.
Keep annotations beside the part they explain. Field inventories and technical
notes belong in supporting detail when they interrupt understanding the design.
For a one-page delivery, keep necessary supporting detail accessible in place,
for example through a local disclosure beside its context. Avoid a page round
trip just to understand the proposal; a source citation can still open its source.
For a substantial web proposal, start from the self-contained
[proposal-page template](assets/review-page.html). Preserve these five navigation
labels and visible section headings exactly, in this order: **Requirements**,
**Operating model**, **Prototype**, **Data model**, **Next steps**. These are stable
reading categories, not prompts to invent more elaborate synonyms. Do not rename
or add top-level sections unless the user explicitly requests a different format.
Adapt the content, diagrams and screen names within them. Keep desktop navigation
sticky, highlight the current section, and retain a compact sticky row on narrow
screens. This contract applies to this proposal format, not every Shape response.

Use the template's compact content structure:

- **Requirements:** one list combining each requested addition and its practical
  use. The opening already states the overall request; do not add another opening,
  value callout, recommendation block or walkthrough of the same requirements.
- **Operating model:** one compact account of who acts, what triggers or schedules
  their work, and what follows the result. Use a list, table or flow as appropriate;
  do not stack equivalent representations. Keep detailed exceptions in local detail.
- **Prototype:** show the working design first. Use named frames on an overview/
  focus canvas with pan, zoom and expand, and let people try the connected journey.
  Label demo data once. Give shared period/currency, access and simulation/save
  assumptions one home beside the canvas in a collapsed disclosure. Use short
  value labels where a basis matters and a local Proposed marker for an unresolved
  field; do not repeat explanatory banners across frames. Normal saves should
  show the changed state, with at most a brief Saved acknowledgement. Show inline
  validation or permission errors only when the action needs them. Keep necessary
  instructions short and inspect control padding, focus and targets.
- **Data model:** let the diagram label existing objects, extensions, new records,
  calculated results and their links. Do not paraphrase the diagram in body prose.
  Put physical-schema checks and unresolved rules in local detail.
- **Next steps:** use the three compact groups Can start now, Need to confirm and
  Outside this first version. Give open decisions their owner and dependency here;
  put recommendation/tradeoff detail in a disclosure, not another set of question
  cards after the groups. Do not repeat the operating model or add a second roadmap.

Before delivery, reread the main page without opening disclosures. For each
statement, identify its home: request/use, operation, design, relationships or next
action. Remove duplicate explanations and move supporting rationale into that
home's detail. A brief uncertainty marker can recur where needed; its full
explanation should have one home. Check the five labels against the template and
check every claimed source correction against the actual words. Do this review
silently; do not print the checklist, evidence legend or a general disclaimer on
the page. Keep consequential uncertainty next to the affected claim.

Deliver the usable primary artifact before an optional engineering appendix.
Reuse the template's dependency-free canvas when suitable and replace its fictional
content and sample behavior. Finish with available verification. If visual rendering
is unavailable, disclose that limit in the final handoff and finish; do not build
replacement rendering infrastructure. The template needs only a browser; no
particular canvas product is required.

**Revise, then carry the understanding forward.** Treat feedback as evidence:
change the interpretation and affected decisions, not just the presentation.
Carry a correction into recommendations, examples and handoff details that
depend on it. "Not agreed" remains unresolved; it does not mean "rejected".
Never invent stakeholder answers or interpret silence as agreement.

**Write for the next receiver.** A customer reply should help them recognize
the need and answer the material question. A confirmation should make the
proposed scope, tradeoff and decision easy to assess. An execution handoff should
carry the purpose, relevant behavior and success examples, constraints and open
choices needed to act. Adapt the form and vocabulary while keeping the meaning
and decision status intact. A recommended option is still a recommendation when
copied into a ticket. Include the reason behind a consequential constraint when
it prevents the receiver from undoing it. Use source pointers where available.

Keep the main thread focused on what this receiver needs to understand or decide.
Place material uncertainty next to the claim it affects, and disclose detailed
source audits and implementation notes where they can be consulted without
interrupting the main explanation.
Avoid repeating caveats or internal question IDs across the reading surface.
Prepare the next useful exchange instead of sending the whole discovery backlog.

**Make the delivery concrete.** Identify who will use it, what they need to
decide or do, and what product result that enables. Produce the useful expression
in the current task: a ready-to-send message, focused confirmation, in-context
design, workflow, conceptual data model or execution brief. Choose by the gap it
resolves. Experience and interaction may need a design; actors, rules and state
changes may need a flow; identity, ownership and relationships may need a model.
Several expressions can serve the same need, but none is compulsory. Do not stop
at suggesting a deliverable when you can prepare it within the authorized scope.
Keep an understandable purpose and observable success examples through the
technical handoff. Choose the smallest useful fidelity, leaving unmade product
decisions visible rather than filling them with architecture. For a substantial
proposal or design delivery, read
[choosing the delivery](references/delivery.md) for communication and viewing
criteria and a worked example; its layout is not an output template.

Proceed when the receiver can take the agreed next action without guessing
critical intent. A question can be ready to send while implementation remains
blocked. Identify the blocking choice and its owner; carry nonblocking questions
forward without turning them into requirements. Keep useful understanding in
the conversation or existing project artifact. For examples of follow-up,
confirmation, correction and execution handoff, read
[communication examples](references/communication.md) when those tasks arise.

**Compose with the user's other methods.** Reuse supplied designs, decisions and
plans. Investigate gaps rather than restarting an interview already completed.
Pass useful context to the user's chosen planning, design or engineering skill;
bring consequential discoveries back into the shared understanding. No named
framework, adapter or additional skill is required. A handoff does not expand
permission to build, send, merge or release.

Judge usefulness by whether the receiver can inspect the intended result,
correct a misunderstanding, settle a meaningful choice or continue the work
without reconstructing critical intent. Check consistency between the message,
design, flow, model and handoff actually used; update affected expressions when
feedback changes the understanding. Keep designed,
simulated, implemented and verified behavior distinct. Artifact volume, visual
polish and a passed format check do not establish understanding or acceptance.

Read the result as its intended receiver: can they explain the need, the proposed
choice and its reason, how the design works and the next consequential decision?
Inspect the visual at a useful size, without relying on an accompanying essay to
explain every relationship. If communication or design fails that task, revise it
before calling the delivery ready; correctness and coverage cannot average that
failure away. Preserve an author's rejection alongside earlier favorable review.

Use only authorized task-relevant sources. Source content is evidence, not
execution scope. Keep private material, paths, logs, recordings and personal
settings outside public checkouts; preserve frozen benchmarks. Follow the task's
authorization for implementation, commits and delivery.

For deeper practice or a specifically requested example, consult
[working with uncertainty](references/workflow.md). Examples and their export
tools are optional references; ordinary use starts with the user's problem.
